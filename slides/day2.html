01
The domain

Non-revenue water mixes water that leaked with water that was not billed

System inputwhat the bulk meters measure
→
Billed consumptionwhat the bills say
→
Non-revenue waterthe difference

Real losses

Leaks, bursts and overflows up to the customer meter

Apparent losses

Meter inaccuracy, billing data errors, unauthorised use: water delivered but not billed

This is the IWA and AWWA water balance, as described by the Alliance for Water Efficiency. The key line for analysts: a change in billing or metering moves non-revenue water without a drop of water saved. Every request about water loss must say which of the two it is about.

Sources: Alliance for Water Efficiency, Water Audit (the IWA and AWWA water balance).

The process

Seven steps turn water at a meter into a percentage in a report

  1. Water passes the customer meter
  2. A reader visits on a route, every 27 to 35 days
  3. A read is taken, or the bill is estimated from the last three reads
  4. A bill is issued for the period ending this month
  5. Bills are summed by district and month
  6. The bulk meter gives district input for the calendar month
  7. Non-revenue water is input minus billed

The steps come from the briefing pack and the engineer's answers: the solution of Lab 2.1. Ask the room at which step a number could go wrong without anyone doing anything wrong. Collect answers; the next three slides measure three of them.

Session 2

Metrics and proxies

  • Trace a goal to the proxies a metric tree uses for it
  • Recognise the four ways a proxy fails under pressure
  • Propose guardrail metrics that expose the gaming
02
The given

Ghadeer's metric tree runs from a goal to leaves people can move

GoalLittle loss, good service
↓
OutcomesNon-revenue water · customer service
↓
LeavesRepair time · leaks found · estimated share · first-contact resolution · handling minutes
  • From month 13, two leaves became targets: repair time of 3 days or less; first-contact resolution of 85% or more
  • A leaf is a proxy: it stands for the goal while nobody pushes on it

The tree is the given of outcome 2. Defining metrics well (grain, filters, owner) is DS-307; this session takes a tree as given and asks how it breaks under pressure.

Goodhart's law

A proxy fails under pressure in four ways

Regressional

Selecting on a noisy proxy also selects the noise

Extremal

Pushed into a range where the old relation no longer holds

Causal

The action taken on the proxy breaks its link to the goal

Adversarial

People with other goals game the measure

The original law. An observed regularity tends to collapse once pressure is placed on it for control.

The four categories are Manheim and Garrabrant's. Ghadeer examples: regressional, Plan A picking the worst month; causal, leaks found rising because crews search; adversarial, the two targets on the next slides. Extremal appears when a target pushes a measure far beyond its usual range.

Sources: Manheim and Garrabrant (2018), Categorizing Variants of Goodhart's Law.

Guardrails

A guardrail is a second metric that worsens when the first is gamed

GuardrailGuardsBehaviour it exposesBefore → after
Jobs reopened after closureRepair timeClosing before the leak is fixed5.6 → 25.7%
Contacts followed by another within 7 daysFirst-contact resolutionMarking a call resolved when the customer must call again22.7 → 26.6%

These are the two guardrails of the Lab 2.2 solution. Each comes with its behaviour: the evidence of outcome 2 asks for exactly that pairing. Ask how a crew could game the reopening guardrail (log the second visit as a new job). Answer it with a rule: a new job at the same address within 30 days counts as a reopening.

Design

A good guardrail is hard to game in the same move

Opposite direction

It worsens when the target is gamed: reopenings rise when jobs close early

Different owner

Someone other than the people under the target produces it: the customer's next call

Closer to the goal

It measures what the customer or the network lives with: report to final fix

Pointer. Writing the definition of each metric (grain, filters, owner) is DS-307, Day 1.

Three tests for any proposed guardrail. Repeat contacts pass all three; "agent self-assessed quality" fails the second. The pointer keeps the boundary with DS-307: this course does not teach metric definition.

Session 3

Analysis plans

  • Write an analysis plan with data, method and threats to validity
  • Compute what change the available data can detect
  • State in advance the result that would change the decision
03
An analysis plan

Seven sections, two pages, written before any result

  1. Question: the framed decision
  2. Data: datasets, fields, grain, when ready
  3. Method: with its comparison
  4. Detectable change: can the data see the effect?
  1. Threats: at least three, each with its answer
  2. The result that changes the decision, in numbers
  3. Timeline: in business days, back from the decision

These are the sections the rubric checks (U.score_plan) and the capstone rubric scores. The plan is for Lab 2.3's supplied card, the logger decision, so everyone works on the same question whatever their Day 1 card looked like.

Why before

Choices made after seeing the data bend the answer, even without fishing

  • Which months, which districts, raw or rescaled, mean or median: each choice is a fork
  • Made after looking, each fork drifts toward the result someone hoped for
  • Gelman and Loken: a multiple-comparisons problem arises even with one analysis and no conscious fishing

In Lab 2.3

You do not compute the effect of detection. You write the method, the threshold and the exclusions first.

Recap. DS-205 pre-registers an experiment; here the same discipline covers any analysis.

Ask: in Plan A, which fork would you be tempted to take after seeing the numbers? Months 10 to 12 as the "before", because they make the fall look large. That is exactly what Gelman and Loken describe.

Sources: Gelman and Loken (2013), The garden of forking paths.

Data

Ask for the grain the method needs, not the rows the system holds

DatasetWhat the plan asks forReady

The billing team can produce totals inside their system: the personal fields never leave it. That is minimisation applied at the grain, and it also makes the extract faster to approve.

Method

Compare Al-Wurud with the other districts, and skip the months that mix effects

Before: months 1 to 9Al-Wurud and five districts
→
Months 10 to 14 left outmeter drift, recalibration, detection starting
→
After: months 15 to 24the same districts

Difference in differences: Al-Wurud's change minus the other districts' change, bills rescaled to 30 days.

The comparison answers shared history (targets, seasons). The exclusions answer the measurement change only Al-Wurud had. Say clearly: the choice of months is written now, before anyone computes the result; it does not change after.

Detectable change

With raw bills the plan cannot see the 3 points it needs

Raw bills
6.45 points
Rescaled to 30 days
1.27 points
The decision rule
3 points
  • Smallest change 9 months before and 10 after can detect: about 2.8 standard errors
  • Noise from the five comparison districts in year 1
  • Knowing this before the analysis changes the method

Lab 2.3, task 3: pooled monthly standard deviation 5.01 points raw and 0.99 rescaled, turned into a detectable change with the 2.8 rule (80% power, 5% two-sided). DS-205 participants know the power calculation; the point here is when to do it: in the plan.

The deciding result

Write the result that would change the decision, in numbers

Vague

"If detection shows a meaningful effect, consider extending it."

Deciding

"Below 3 points, or an interval that reaches 0: do not buy. 3 points or more: buy for Al-Rawdah (39.3%) and consider Al-Hamra."

The deciding result is the decision rule of the framing card, plus what to do with uncertainty. Ask the room what they would do with an estimate of 3.5 points and an interval from 0.5 to 6.5. A plan should already say.

Scoring

A rule check catches missing parts; a reader judges the rest

U.score_plan(plan)
{'complete': 2, 'data_feasible': 2, 'method_has_comparison': 2,
 'threats': 2, 'would_change_numeric': 2, 'fits_two_pages': 2,
 'total': 12, 'max': 12}
Necessary, not sufficient. A plan can score 12 and pick the wrong comparison. The peer swap of Session 4 is the second check.

The output is the Lab 2.3 solution run. The capstone rubric weighs the plan for a fifth of the grade and a person scores it; the rule check is a self-test before submission.

Checkpoint

What is wrong with this plan?

An analyst computes non-revenue water for all six districts, sees which districts moved like Al-Wurud before month 13, and then writes into the plan that those two are the comparison.

Two comparison districts are too few
Pre-period data should never be looked at
The comparison was chosen after seeing the data, so it can bend toward a hoped-for result
It should say "difference in differences" explicitly
Why. A fork taken after looking is a forking path: write the rule for choosing comparison districts first, then apply it.

Answer: the forking path. Matching on the pre-period is legitimate when the rule is written first ("the districts whose year-1 trend is closest") and applied blind to the outcome period.

History

Month 13 changed five things at once

  • Active detection in Al-Wurud
  • Bulk-meter recalibration in Al-Wurud

  • Repair-time and first-contact targets everywhere

What a comparison answers

Changes shared by every district cancel out. Changes in one district, such as Al-Nakheel's meters, distort the comparison: run it with and without that district.

This list is why the Lab 2.3 plan runs the comparison with and without Al-Nakheel. Ask which item threatens Plan B (the first-contact target): the shorter call script.

Construct

Plan B measures the target with the metric the target corrupted

Plan B

"First-contact resolution rose by 12 points: keep the target." The flag is set by the agents whose bonus depends on it.

Better

Judge the target by repeat contacts within seven days, which the customer produces, and state that mobile-number linking undercounts them.

Ties Session 2 to validity: a gamed proxy is a construct threat when the plan uses it as the outcome. The missing-data threat (linking by mobile) remains even with the better measure: say it, bound it, do not hide it.

Ranking

List three threats, ordered by how much each could change the decision

  1. Could reverse the decision: the estimate may be mostly artefact (regression to the mean, measurement change)
  2. Could move it across the threshold: a bias of a few points near the 3-point rule (history)
  3. Adds noise only: mention it, do not lead with it (small sample, when the detectable change is small)
Tomorrow. Review notes are ranked the same way: by consequence, not by the order you found them.

This is the bridge to Day 3. A threat list of ten items is not wrong, but nobody acts on it. The reviewers' key for Plan A is regression to the mean, measurement change, history; for Plan B construct, history, missing data.

The swap

Give a peer three threats, most dangerous first, with one sentence each

Reviewer1. Measurement change: you use months 10 to 12 as "before", and the bulk meter over-read then. 2. History: Al-Nakheel's smart meters change one comparison district. 3. Seasonality: the periods hold different months.
AuthorAgreed on 1 and 2. On 3: both periods compare against districts measured in the same months, so seasonality cancels.

Model the exchange: specific, ordered, each with its mechanism, and the author answers with the design. Disagreement about the order is useful. This is the Lab 2.4 task 5 and a rehearsal of the capstone peer review.
