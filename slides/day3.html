Session 1

A review checklist

  • Rerun an analysis before judging it
  • Apply a checklist of five areas from question fit to communication
  • Score an analysis so another reviewer would score it alike
01
The first step

A review starts by running the analysis, not by reading it

Rerundoes it run, from the supplied data?
→
Reconciledoes every sentence match the output above it?
→
Check five areasfrom question to communication
What goes wrong. The reviewer reads a fluent text, agrees, and never sees that the code prints something else.

The order matters: rerunning first stops the prose from setting your expectations. In Lab 3.1 the rerun alone catches one of the flaws.

The checklist

Five areas, from the question to the words, scored 0 to 2

Area210
Question fitAnswers the decision askedA nearby questionAnother question

The five areas are those of outcome 5. Anchored levels are what make two reviewers agree: a score without a sentence of evidence is an opinion. DS-407 later turns such a checklist into a team standard; here it is a personal tool.

The score

Two points out of ten, each with its sentence

The reference score of Lab 3.1. The lab accepts a score within one point per area. Where a participant is two points away, ask for their sentence: usually they read the text and skipped the output.

Calibration

A checklist is useful when two reviewers land within a point

Anchors

What a 2, a 1 and a 0 look like, written down before scoring

Evidence

One sentence per score that points at a cell or a number

Compare

Two reviewers score alone, then discuss only the gaps of two points

Pointer. Team review standards (who reviews what, sign-off rules) are DS-407.

Calibration is cheap: ten minutes on a shared notebook once a quarter. The pointer marks the boundary set by the catalog: this course is about the individual reviewer's judgement.

Checkpoint

Which area does this flaw belong to?

An analysis of estimated bills prints "estimated share: 16.4%" for Al-Hamra. The summary below says "about one bill in four is estimated in Al-Hamra".

Data handling
Communication: the words misstate the output
Method
Inference
Why. The number is right and the sentence is wrong: one in six, not one in four. Only a reviewer who reads the output against the words catches it.

Answer: communication. A borderline case: some will say inference. Accept the discussion, and point to the anchors: inference is overreach from evidence; communication is the reader being misled about what the evidence is.

Session 2

Finding the flaws that matter

  • Locate seeded flaws cell by cell
  • Measure each flaw's consequence with a recomputed number
  • Rank flaws by their effect on the decision
02
The pool

Seven kinds of flaw, four in each variant

Kind of flawArea
F1Success measured by an activity, not the outcomeQuestion fit
F2A join on a text key silently drops rowsData handling
F3Before-after in one unit, no comparison, months that mix changesMethod
F4Significance or certainty claimed with no intervalInference
F5Volumes not adjusted for the billing periodData handling
F6A chart axis that does not start at zeroCommunication
F7Estimated bills dropped from billed volumeData handling

Every participant's variant of Sabah's analysis holds four of these, always with at least one of F1 or F3. F7 also flips the decision on its own (2.34 to 4.16 points in the Lab 3.2 run), so a participant who holds it has more than one decision-flipping flaw to rank first. The pool is public; which four you have is not. Each kind was met on Days 1 and 2: the review uses that domain knowledge.

Consequence

The consequence of a flaw is what it does to the answer, measured

The analysis with no flawthe reference answer
→
The analysis with only this flawthe flawed answer
→
The differencepoints moved, and does the decision flip?
U.consequence(MY_NUMBER, "F3")
# clean 2.34 → flawed 4.12 points; decision: do not buy → buy

Isolating one flaw at a time keeps flaws from masking each other: with F1 present, no flaw changes the printed number, because the headline is about leaks. In real reviews you rarely have the clean version; you estimate the consequence by fixing the flaw and rerunning, as the stretch does.

The Lab 3.2 run of variant 0, the one you demonstrate. F2 drops about a third of Al-Wurud's repair rows and changes the effect by nothing: the effect comes from the water balance. A reviewer who leads with F2 because it was the first found has the order wrong.

The answer

Without the flaws, detection cut non-revenue water by 2.3 points: do not buy

2.3
points, 95% interval 1.4 to 3.3, against the other districts without Al-Nakheel
  • The plan of Lab 2.3 fixed the rule before: below 3 points, do not buy
  • Sabah's notebook said buy, on leaks found rising 3.4 times
  • The fall is real but small: worth knowing, not worth the loggers at this price

Reveal the prediction from the Day 2 review checkpoint. The interval reaches above 3, so say it plainly: the data are consistent with a benefit up to 3.3 points, and the rule written in advance says do not buy. That is what pre-commitment is for.

Ranking

Rank by consequence, not by the order you found the flaws

  1. Flips the decision: F1 and F3 in variant 0; F7 too where it appears (+1.8 points, past the rule)
  2. Moves the number, by how many points: none here; F5 moves it by 0.02 in other variants
  3. Misleads without moving a number: an axis, an unstated uncertainty
  4. No effect on this answer: still worth a note if it can bite elsewhere (the join)

This is the ranking rule the lab checks. Flaws found first are usually the easiest to see (a chart, a spelling), which is exactly why the order of finding is a poor order of reporting.

Authenticity

Each participant reviews a variant of their own, checked without a visible key

  • Your participant number picks four flaws from the pool
  • U.flags_correct compares a hash of your flags with the variant's
  • Right or wrong, without revealing which flaws you missed

In the capstone

The assessed review uses unseen variants made for each participant; review notes are matched against your own seeded flaws.

The lab's variants are practice: their generator is in the public repository. The capstone variants are prepared by the trainer outside the repository and are never published. Tell participants this openly; it removes the temptation and explains the design.

The missing interval
The before-after with no comparison

Session 3

Reviewing assistant-generated analysis

  • Name the failures typical of assistant-generated analyses
  • Rerun an assistant's notebook and reconcile its summary with its output
  • Write a verification note that meets the duty to verify
03
What to expect

An assistant's analysis looks finished; check five things before believing it

  • It may not run: a column or a function that does not exist
  • Its numbers may not come from its code: a summary written beside the output
  • Outside numbers with no source: benchmarks, norms
  • The direction of a change reversed in the words
  • The method answers an easier question than the one asked
Recap. Recomputing and running a request twice are DS-206; here you review someone else's assistant-generated work.

The perishable part of this session: which assistants are used and their typical failures change each edition. The five checks are durable. Lab 3.3 meets four of them at once.

The case

Sareea's summary reversed the direction of the change

Al-Nakheel, billed m3 per account per monthBeforeAfterChange
Sareea's summary21.419.6-8.2%
Recomputed from reads.csv25.828.7+11.5%
And. The notebook stops at cell s2 (no column "consumption"), and the "industry benchmark of 15%" has no source.

Lab 3.3 run. None of the summary's numbers can be found in the data, and the notebook as delivered could never have produced them. Ask: how many of you would have forwarded the summary had it arrived with a chart?

Four checks

All four checks fail, each with its evidence

CheckResultEvidence
Reruns as deliveredfailKeyError: "consumption" is not a column
Every number traced to outputfail21.4, 19.6 and -8.2% appear nowhere
Outside numbers sourcedfailThe 15% benchmark has no source
Method fits the questionfailBills measure what the meters register, not what people use

The last check is the one participants miss: even the corrected computation answers "did bills change", not "did consumption change". That is Day 2's measurement change, met again in an assistant's output.

The national guideline

Verifying assistant output before use is a duty, not a courtesy

  • Identify content generated with generative AI
  • Check it for factual accuracy, especially in official reports
  • Verify generated code before using or running it

Source

SDAIA's Generative AI Guidelines for Government, in its sections on reliability and on users' responsibilities.

Paraphrased, not quoted: the guideline asks users to identify generated content, proofread it for accuracy, verify outputs particularly for official reports, and verify generated code before executing it. The verification note of the lab is how a reviewer shows that duty was met.

Sources: SDAIA, Generative AI Guidelines for Government.

The deliverable

A verification note says what may be used, what may not, and what next

Status

Not verified: do not use the summary

May be used

Billed m3 per account in Al-Nakheel rose from 25.8 to 28.7 (+11.5%) after the smart meters

May not be used

The 8.2% fall, the 15% benchmark, the roll-out advice

Next

Measure use another way (night flow at the district meter), against districts without smart meters

The note ends with a label: assistant-generated, by whom, verified by whom, and with what result. A verified analysis keeps its label too: the reader deserves to know how it was made.

Checkpoint

What do you do with this summary?

An assistant's notebook runs, and its summary's three numbers all appear in the output. The summary concludes that smart meters "cut leakage in Al-Nakheel by nine points".

Withhold it: the numbers are right but the method measures billing, not leakage
Accept it: every number traces to the output
Accept it once a chart is added
Reject it because an assistant wrote it
Why. Passing three checks is not passing four: non-revenue water fell because the new meters bill more, so the claim about leakage is a measurement change.

Answer: withhold it. The last option matters: assistant-generated is a reason to verify, not to reject. The checks apply to any analysis.

Session 4

Review notes people act on

  • Rewrite a vague note as problem, consequence and fix
  • Order notes by consequence for the decision
  • Recommend the kind of analysis the request needed
04
One note, twice

A note gets acted on when it names the problem, the consequence and the fix

Weak

"The join looks off."

Acted on

Problem: cell c2 joins repairs on the district name; rows spelt "Al Wurud" are dropped. Consequence: about a third of Al-Wurud's repairs vanish from the count. Fix: join on a district code and assert the row count.

Note N1 of Lab 3.4, before and after. The weak note is right: it is simply useless to the author, who cannot tell where, how much or what to do.

The parts

Three parts, each a full sentence

Problem

Where, and exactly what: the cell, the line, the number

Consequence

What it does to the number or the decision, in points or in the choice

Fix

An action: "join on the code", not "consider the join"

Tone. Write about the analysis, not the analyst: "cell c3 compares" rather than "you compared".

The lab's helper flags a note whose consequence names neither a number nor the decision, and a fix that starts with "consider" or "maybe". Tone matters because notes are read by the author first: a defensive author fixes less.

Eight notes, ranked by what they do to the decision

The first lines

A review opens with its verdict on the decision

For Nadia

The Lab 3.4 stretch. Three sentences: verdict, main reason with its number, what happens next. Everything else is the numbered notes below it.

After the review

A review can conclude that the request needed another kind of analysis

  • Sareea's request asked a causal question and got a description of bills
  • The right answer is a causal analysis against districts without smart meters, on a measure of use
  • Sometimes the right recommendation is no analysis yet: the data cannot reach the question by the date

In the capstone defence

You recommend the kind of analysis, and defend it when the sponsor changes a constraint.

Outcome 6 closes the loop with Day 1, Session 2. The capstone rubric gives this recommendation its own criterion. A changed constraint (a frozen budget, a shorter deadline) often moves it from "experiment" to "causal analysis" or to "no analysis now".

Checkpoint

Which note will the author act on?

Four notes on the same flaw in Sareea's analysis, which treats estimated bills as actual reads.

"What about estimated reads?"
"Use actual reads only."
"You have overstated the change."
"Cell s2 averages estimated bills as reads, so the change mixes billing timing with metering; recompute on actual reads and report the estimated share."

Day 3 · wrap-up

Three things to keep

Rerun, then read

Run the analysis, reconcile every sentence with its output, then score five areas.

Rank by consequence

A flaw that flips the decision goes first, whatever order you found it in.

Verify, label, then use

Assistant output passes four checks and carries its label before anyone acts on it.

Close the teaching days by returning to the opening question of Day 1: an analysis nobody used. Now participants can frame it so it is usable, plan it so it is sound, and review it so it is trusted.
