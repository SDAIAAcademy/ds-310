Session 1

Requests are not questions

  • Separate the decision behind a request from the topic it names
  • State an estimand with population, variable, contrast and summary
  • Write a decision rule a peer can apply without asking you
01
The request

A request names a worry; a question names a decision

Mamdouh, request R4The acoustic-logger pilot in Al-Wurud found lots of leaks. Should we buy loggers for more districts?
Analyst, first reflexI will count the leaks the loggers found and compare districts.
Analyst, framedShould Ghadeer buy loggers for Al-Rawdah and Al-Hamra next year? Buy if detection cut non-revenue water by at least 3 points in Al-Wurud, relative to the other districts.

Read the request aloud. Ask the room what the first reflex would produce: a count of leaks found. Then ask who would act on that count, and how. The framed version names a choice, a measure of the outcome and the number at which the choice flips. That is the whole of Session 1 in one exchange.

The given

Five requests, five missing decisions

RequestFromWhat is missing
R1 "Water loss in Al-Rawdah is out of control"NadiaThe choice the board faces
R2 "Look into the estimated bills"KhalilA threshold for acting
R3 "Find out what is going on" with repeatsSultanaEverything but the topic
R4 "Should we buy loggers for more districts?"MamdouhThe outcome: water, not leaks found
R5 "Do the smart meters work?"NadiaWork at what: saving water or measuring it?

These are the five requests of outcome 1. Lab 1.1 frames R4 and R2; the stretch frames R5. R3 is the interview of Session 3. R1 returns in Session 4. Point out that R4 already contains a decision ("should we buy") but measures it with the wrong thing.

Before the data

The decision comes first: who chooses, between what

Decision maker

A person with the authority and the budget: Nadia, with the capital committee

The decision

A question that ends in a choice: "Should ...?", "Which ...?"

Options

At least two, and "do nothing" among them: every option is judged against it

Recap. DS-308 Day 1 frames a decision for a memo; DS-204 Day 1 frames a prediction for a decision. Here the frame goes further: an estimand and a rule.

Participants who took DS-308 or DS-204 know the first three boxes; keep this to a minute. The new part is what follows: the estimand fixes exactly what number answers the decision, and the rule fixes what that number must be.

The estimand

An estimand states exactly which number answers the question

PartQuestion it answersR4, framed
PopulationWhich units, which period?District-months of six districts, months 1 to 24
VariableWhat is measured on each unit?Non-revenue water, % of system input
ContrastWith what, against what?Al-Wurud with detection against five districts without
Intercurrent eventsWhat happens on the way that changes the meaning?The bulk meter was recalibrated in month 13
SummaryWhich one number?Difference in differences, in points

The five attributes come from the ICH E9(R1) addendum on estimands, written for clinical trials and now used well beyond them. Stress the intercurrent event: the Al-Wurud bulk meter over-read for three months and was fixed in the month detection started, so a careless before-after counts a meter repair as a leak saving.

Sources: ICH E9(R1) Addendum on estimands (2019).

The rule

A decision rule names the number at which the choice changes

Not a rule

"Buy loggers if detection works well."
"Act if many bills are estimated."

A rule

"Buy for a district if detection cut non-revenue water by at least 3 points and the district is above 25%."
"Book second visits if more than 10% of Al-Hamra bills are estimated."

Why write it now. A threshold set after the result is seen bends to the result. Al-Hamra's share is 16.4% (Lab 1.1): the rule was set before anyone looked.

Ask where the thresholds come from: cost (what a district's loggers cost against the water they save), capacity, or policy. If nobody can say, the rule is a placeholder and the card says so. The 16.4% is the Lab 1.1 run over two years.

Responsible framing

Every card says who is affected and whether personal data are needed at all

  • Affected: customers facing interruptions, readers, crews, the budget that funds something else
  • Personal data: a district total answers R4; names, mobiles and national IDs do not help
  • Decide it on the card, before the data request is written

The rule behind it

The Personal Data Protection Law limits collected personal data to the minimum the purpose needs; its implementing regulation asks for a map linking each item to a purpose.

This is a design step, not a compliance afterthought. Paraphrase only: Article 11 of the PDPL limits content to the minimum necessary for the purpose, and Article 19 of the implementing regulation repeats data minimisation with data maps. Do not read clauses aloud from memory.

Sources: Personal Data Protection Law; Implementing Regulation of the PDPL.

The test of a card

A card is done when a peer can plan from it without asking you

U.card_gaps({"decision": "Look into the estimated bills",
             "decision_rule": "Act if the share is high"})
# What is the decision maker?  What are the options?
# Is the decision written as a question that ends in a choice?
# Estimand: what is the population? ... the summary?
# Decision rule: at what number does the choice change?
# What does 'high' mean here, in a number?

The helper is a rule check, not a judge: it lists the questions a planner would still ask. In the lab, aim for an empty list, then hand the card to a neighbour and ask what data they would fetch first. If they hesitate, the card is not done.

Checkpoint

Which gap matters most in this card?

Khalil's card reads: "Measure how accurate Al-Hamra's bills were in 2026, using all billing data, and report the results to management." What should be added first?

A longer period, so the estimate is more precise
The decision it informs and the number at which that decision changes
A chart template for the management report
A model of which bills will be inaccurate next year
Why. Without a decision and a rule, any result is "interesting" and none is acted on; the other gaps can wait until the decision is known.

Answer: the decision and the threshold. If many pick the longer period, ask what Khalil would do differently with a more precise number. If they pick the model, point out the request is about the past year: a prediction answers another question.

Session 2

Question types

  • Classify a request as descriptive, predictive, causal or unanswerable
  • Match each type to description, an experiment, a causal analysis, a model or no analysis
  • Change the recommendation when a constraint changes
02
Four labels

Each kind of question needs a different kind of analysis

LabelThe request asksIt needs
DescriptiveWhat happened, how much, whereA description
PredictiveWhat will happen to each unitA predictive model
CausalWhat a change did, or would doAn experiment if one can still run; otherwise a causal analysis of existing data
UnanswerableSomething the data cannot reach, or no decisionNo analysis yet: reframe or collect

These four labels are the course's working set and the ones the assessment uses. The next slide shows a finer map from the literature. Emphasise the last row: "no analysis" is a professional recommendation, not a failure.

The commonest error

Most analysis errors start by mistaking the type of question

  • Leek and Peng list six types: descriptive, exploratory, inferential, predictive, causal, mechanistic
  • They call mistaking the type the most common error in data analysis
Real typeTaken forThe error has a name
InferentialCausalCorrelation read as causation
ExploratoryInferentialData dredging
ExploratoryPredictiveOverfitting
DescriptiveInferentialAn "n of 1" analysis

The table is from Leek and Peng's one-page article in Science. Ask for a Ghadeer example of the first row: "districts with more loggers have fewer leaks, so loggers prevent leaks" when loggers went where crews already were.

Sources: Leek and Peng (2015), What is the question? Science 347(6228).

Words mislead

"Which" can open a description or a prediction

C1 · descriptive

"How many leak repairs did each district have last quarter?" A count of the past.

C2 · predictive

"Which accounts are likely to get an estimated bill next month?" A forecast per account.

C5 · predictive

"Which mains are most likely to burst next summer?" A forecast per main.

Ask two things. Past or future? The whole group, or each unit?

These are three of the eight Lab 1.2 requests. The two questions in the callout sort most requests in seconds. C5 also needs the pipe-network data, which is Secret: that matters on Session 4, not for the label.

Causal questions

An experiment only when the change can still be assigned at random

C4 · text before the reader's visitNot done yet: randomise accounts, an experiment
→
C8 · smart meters in one districtDone, one district: causal analysis against similar districts
→
C3 · target in every team at onceDone, everywhere: causal analysis of existing data, weakest
Recap. Designing and sizing the experiment is DS-205; here you decide whether one is possible.

Read the flow left to right as decreasing control. DS-205 participants know which claims need an experiment; the new skill here is to see that the choice is often already made by history. C3 has no untreated group at all: say so in the recommendation.

The fourth label

"Unanswerable" is an answer, if it comes with a better question

C6 as asked

"Why do customers distrust us?" No decision, and no data on trust.

C6 reframed

"Which contact reasons are followed by a repeat contact within seven days most often, so we fix the top two first?"

  • Say what data would make it answerable, and what decision it would serve
  • Do not produce a chart that pretends to answer the original

The reframed version is the Lab 1.2 stretch. It is answerable from the contact log, and it serves a decision (which two reasons to fix). The original could only be answered by a survey or interviews, and still has no decision.

The meeting moves

When a constraint changes, the recommendation changes with it

NadiaThe text-message budget is frozen until next year, and the board wants a decision on meter reading in ten business days. No new trial can run before then.
AnalystThen C4 gets no analysis now: decide the reading schedule on cost and practicality, and run the reminder experiment when the budget returns.
In the capstone. The sponsor changes one constraint during your talk. You revise out loud.

This is outcome 6 in miniature and a rehearsal for the defence. Ask the room which other requests the message affects. Answer: none; C4 is the only one that needed a new trial. Someone may argue for a causal analysis of old data: there are no old reminders, so there is nothing to analyse.

Process models

CRISP-DM starts with the business; it does not frame the question for you

Business understandingthe only phase about the decision
↓
Data understanding, preparation, modeling, evaluation, deploymentfive phases about the data and the model
  • The model is a cycle of six phases: useful as a checklist of stages
  • It assumes a model will be built: no place for "description is enough" or "no analysis"
  • It says little about stakeholders, estimands or when to stop

Use CRISP-DM as a map of where framing sits, not as a method of framing. The phase list is in IBM's CRISP-DM guide. Its limit for this course: it presumes a modelling project, while half of Ghadeer's requests need a description or nothing.

Checkpoint

What does this request need?

In month 13 Ghadeer moved meter reading in all six districts to a new contractor. Khalil asks: "Did the new contractor cut the number of estimated bills?" The decision is whether to renew the contract.

A description of estimated bills by month
An experiment that randomises districts
A causal analysis of existing data, stating its weaknesses
A predictive model of estimated bills
Why. It asks what a change did, the change is already made everywhere, and a decision depends on it: a causal analysis of existing data, with no untreated group named as its main weakness.

Answer: the causal analysis. Those who pick the description have the right data and the wrong question type; ask them what they would tell Khalil about the contractor specifically. Those who pick the experiment: ask which district could still be assigned to the old contractor.

Session 3

Interviewing a stakeholder

  • Map decision makers, users and affected groups for a request
  • Ask open questions that surface constraints and acceptable evidence
  • Record what was said apart from what was assumed
03
Who to talk to

Map who decides, who uses the answer and who is affected

Decide

Sultana, with Nadia for the call-centre contract

Use

Team leaders; the outsourced call-centre supplier

Affected

Customers who call again; agents whose bonus rides on a target; older customers with estimated bills

What goes wrong. Only the decider is interviewed, and the affected groups appear for the first time in a complaint.

This is the map participants draw before the Lab 1.3 interview, for request R3. The affected column is where the ethics of the analysis live: the agents' bonuses and the older customers in Al-Hamra both shape what a fair answer looks like.

How you ask

Open questions find constraints; closed and leading ones find agreement

LeadingDon't you think it's the agents?
SultanaYes, I suppose so.
OpenHow do you count a repeat contact today: how do you know it is the same customer?
SultanaThe system links contacts by mobile number. When a customer calls from another phone, it is a new customer to us.

Both answers come from the Lab 1.3 stand-in. The second one changes the analysis: repeat rates are a lower bound. Ask the room to turn "Is it the billing calls?" into an open question ("Which reasons do repeat callers give?").

What to ask

Six topics cover most of what a stakeholder knows and you do not

  1. The decision and its date: what will you do differently, and by when?
  2. How the number is counted: how do you know it is a repeat, a leak, an estimate?
  3. Acceptable evidence: what would convince you, and what would you not trust?
  4. Incentives: which targets do people work under?
  5. What changed: what moved in the last year?
  6. Rules on data: what may be used, by whom, where?

The list is a scaffold, not a script: follow the surprises. Topics 2 and 4 are the ones analysts skip, and they hide the two largest traps of Day 2 (how data are produced, and how targets distort them).

What nobody volunteers

Five constraints changed the analysis before it started

Heard in the interviewWhat it changes
Contract renewal in four weeksThe deadline, and the decision

Contacts linked by mobile numberRepeats from another phone are missed

Each row is one of the five constraints in Sultana's role card; the stand-in reports how many your questions reached. With a live role-player, hand out the card after the interview and compare. Ask which row would have hurt most if missed: usually the bonus target.

The record

Write what you heard apart from what you assumed

Heard

"We shortened the call script in month 13. Handling time fell a lot."

Assumed

That repeat contacts rose after month 13. Not yet checked in the data.

Why it matters. An assumption written as a fact becomes a finding by Day 3, and nobody remembers it was a guess.

The record has two columns on purpose. Ask the room what the shorter script might do to repeats: shorter calls may resolve fewer problems. That is an assumption to test, not a fact. Day 2 tests it.

Before the analysis

Agree the evidence before you produce it

  • Sultana trusts repeat contacts within seven days
  • She does not trust the satisfaction survey: few people answer it
  • An analysis built on the survey would be ignored, however good

On the framing card

The variable of the estimand is the evidence the decision maker accepts; if it is not, say why in the card.

Link back to Session 1: the estimand's variable. Acceptable evidence is not the same as good evidence: if the accepted measure is flawed (repeats linked by mobile), the card says so and proposes how to bound the flaw.

The lab

The interview is a role-play; the stand-in is for practice

In the room

The trainer or a neighbour holds Sultana's role card and answers only what is asked. Eight minutes, then swap.

Alone

U.interview("sultana", q) answers by topic, gives agreement to a leading question, and counts the constraints you reached.

Role card rules for the role-player: answer truthfully, never volunteer a constraint, answer a closed question with yes or no, agree with a leading question. The stand-in follows the same rules mechanically; it is cruder than a person but gives instant feedback for self-study.

Checkpoint

Which question finds out how a repeat is counted?

You suspect the repeat-contact rate is undercounted, but you do not know how the system links contacts. Which question do you ask Sultana?

"How does the system decide that two contacts come from the same customer?"
"Is the repeat rate accurate?"
"Surely some repeats are missed, right?"
"Do you trust the repeat-contact figures?"
Why. An open question about the process makes her describe the mechanism (linking by mobile number), and the mechanism tells you the direction of the error.

Answer: the first. Ask those who chose "do you trust" what they would do with "yes". The point of interviewing for constraints is to learn mechanisms, not opinions.

Minimisation

Ask for what the purpose needs, not what happens to be ready

Tempting

"Billing reads are ready in two days: take them, we might link bursts to customers later."

Minimised

"The burst question needs repairs and temperature. Billing brings names, mobiles and national IDs it does not need."

On the card. Every dataset kept has a reason tied to the question; every personal field dropped has one too.

"We might need it later" is the phrase to listen for. Under the PDPL the purpose sets the minimum; a later purpose is a later request. Ask: what would linking bursts to customers decide? Usually nothing on this question.

Cutting scope

Cut the question until it fits, and say what the cut leaves out

Asked (causal)Do road works explain the bursts in Al-Rawdah, and should renewal go to its oldest mains?
↓
Scoped (descriptive)Have bursts in Al-Rawdah become more frequent over 24 months, and do they follow summer temperatures, enough to justify a renewal study?

Lab 1.4 run: 4.7 repairs a month in year 1, 4.9 in year 2; 4.8 in summer and 4.8 otherwise.

The scoped question is smaller and honest about its type. The numbers from the run already hint at the answer: no clear rise, no summer pattern. That is a useful result for the board: it says the renewal decision needs the pipe and road-works data, so request them today.

The cost of the cut

A scoped card says what is left unanswered and requests it now

  • Left unanswered: whether road works or pipe age cause the bursts
  • Requested today: road-works permits through a data-sharing request; the pipe network through the security approval
  • Told to the sponsor: the board gets a description now and a causal answer in about a month
What goes wrong. The scoped answer is presented as the answer to the original question.

This slide closes Day 1's framing arc: decision, type, stakeholders, feasibility. In the capstone, the plan must contain exactly this kind of statement, and the sponsor's constraint change usually lands on it.

Checkpoint

How do you answer this data request?

Planning the burst analysis, a colleague asks for the billing extract with national IDs "in case we want to link bursts to customers later". The question is about bursts by month.

Take it: it is ready in two days
Take it, but hash the national IDs
Refuse it: Restricted data cannot be analysed
Leave it out: the burst question needs no personal data; a later purpose gets its own request
Why. Minimisation ties the data to the purpose. No part of the burst question needs customers, so no personal field enters the plan.

Answer: leave it out. Hashing is a good control when personal data are needed; here they are not needed at all. The Restricted option is a frequent confusion: Restricted data are used every day inside Ghadeer's environment.
